*2 October 2026*

My World Heritage uses a simple temple silhouette to signify cultural places and artefacts. The project makes this artwork available to everyone through a public-domain dedication. You can use it on a map, in a collection catalogue, beside a museum entry, or in another application without asking this project for permission.

# Why an independent symbol

An official emblem carries meaning beyond the subject it depicts. On an independent application, it can suggest that an institution operates, approves or endorses the service. Respecting that distinction helps visitors understand whose work they are using.

UNESCO requires prior written authorisation to use its logo [1]. The World Heritage Emblem has its own rules: the World Heritage Centre directs prospective users to obtain authorisation through the appropriate National Commission [2]. Publishing information about heritage does not by itself grant permission to brand that publication with either emblem.

The Hague Convention Blue Shield has a different purpose again. It identifies cultural property and people within the Convention's protection arrangements, with distinct forms for different protection categories [3]. Treating it as a generic decorative heritage icon would obscure that purpose.

These are reasons to respect the official symbols and their conditions of use, rather than copy them into an unrelated product. Authorised uses exist; this project does not claim such authorisation or institutional endorsement.

# The shared artwork

The mark in *Figure Shared heritage mark* consists of a triangular roof, three plain columns and a base. It uses the familiar architectural idea of a temple to suggest cultural heritage. It does not reproduce UNESCO lettering, the World Heritage Emblem's composition or the Blue Shield. Its intended meaning is broad enough to include cultural artefacts as well as buildings; it does not certify a site's inscription, protection status or authenticity.
